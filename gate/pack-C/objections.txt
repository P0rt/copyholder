Before engines, much of the flour in the countryside was ground by windmills. A typical mill sat alone on a low hill outside the village, where nothing blocked the wind, and its four long sails could be seen for miles. On a clear spring morning they turned slowly against a wide sky of deep [[brown|blue]], with only a few white clouds drifting past. Inside, the tower had several floors, joined by steep ladders, and the floors were dusty with flour.

The miller could turn the whole cap of the mill with a long pole, so that the sails always faced straight into the wind. On a day with no wind at all, which often happened in late summer, the sails [[spun fast|stood still]] for hours on end. Then the miller mended torn sacks in the yard, or swept the floors.

Farmers brought their grain to the mill by horse and cart, often from villages many miles away. While the sacks were being ground, their horses rested in the shade of the old barn and were fed with [[meat|hay]] from the loft above the stable. The farmers waited on a bench in the yard and talked about the weather, the prices and the coming harvest.

Inside the mill, the sails drove a long wooden shaft that ran down the middle of the tower, and the shaft turned the gears. The heavy gears drove the millstones, which crushed the grain into the flour that bakers use for [[soup|bread]], and it ran down a wooden chute into the sacks below. The miller tied each sack tightly with string when it was full and stacked it by the door, ready for the waiting cart.

Many of the farmers also kept large flocks of sheep on the hills nearby. Once every year, in spring, the sheep were sheared for their [[feathers|wool]], which was spun into yarn by the fire.

The miller weighed every load and kept a careful record. Payment was often taken in grain rather than coins, a share from every sack.

One busy day a farmer brought three sacks in the morning and three more after lunch. Together that made [[eleven sacks|six sacks]] in all, and the miller wrote them down in the book. At the end of each month the share was added up, and some of it was sold to the bakers in the nearby town.

The mill was always busiest just after the harvest, but grain arrived at the mill all the year round. In the warmest season of the year, [[winter|summer]], the doors of the mill stood open from dawn to dusk, and flour dust drifted over the yard.

The miller lived in a house beside the mill with a large family. There was no tap in the old kitchen, so every morning the children walked across the yard to the deep well by the garden gate. They carried the water back in [[nets|buckets]], one in each hand, and filled the barrel by the door.

Behind the house, the garden was full of good things to eat, all grown by the family. There were rows of beans, peas and onions, and a pear tree by the fence.

The strawberries ripened first, and when they were ready to pick, they were [[black|red]] and sweet, and the children ate as many as they picked. Pears came later, in the autumn, and were stored in the attic on beds of clean straw.

Visitors could always find the old mill, because its sails rose high above the wide, flat fields. Over the door hung a painted wooden sign in the shape of a triangle, with [[twenty sides|three sides]], and a picture in the middle. It showed a full sack of flour and a fresh loaf of bread.

Near the garden stood a long row of beehives. On warm days the bees flew out over the meadows, and they came back heavy with pollen from the clover and wild flowers. Late in the year the hives were opened, and the bees had made [[cheese|honey]] enough for the whole family to last the winter. In good years the farms nearby grew more than enough wheat, barley and rye for the mill. In bad years, when the harvest failed, the price of bread rose, and grain had to be brought from far away, at great cost. Some of it came in heavy sacks by ship across the Atlantic [[River|Ocean]], and then by cart from the harbor. The journey took many weeks, and the sacks often arrived damp and had to be spread out in the yard before grinding.

Winters on the hill were long and bitter, and the cold wind howled day after day around the tower. Each year around the middle of winter, the days were always much [[longer|shorter]] than in summer, and the cow stayed in the barn for weeks. The children kept the stable doors shut, and the miller wrapped the gears in thick sacking against the frost. The busy months came and went, and the family grew older, but the work at the mill hardly changed at all. A grey cat slept by the kitchen stove all through the day and hunted [[bears|mice]] at night. Year after year the mill kept on turning, until engines took over and most of the old mills stood idle for good.

The last miller on the hill retired long ago, and the house beside the mill was sold to a family from the town. Today only a few of these mills still turn. Some have been restored by volunteers, who grind flour on open days and show visitors how the sails, the gears and the stones work together. Children climb the steep ladders to the cap and look out over the fields where the wagons once came and went.
