Every river begins as rain or snow high in the hills. Some of the water soaks into the ground and seeps through cracks in the rock. Where it comes back to the surface, it forms a spring. Other water runs straight off the land in thin trickles after a storm.

Trickles join together and become small streams. Streams join other streams, and with each meeting the flow grows larger. The water takes the lowest path it can find, so it gathers in the folds between hills and carves them deeper over time.

In the hills, a young river is fast and narrow. It tumbles over stones and drops over small waterfalls. It carries sand and pebbles along with it, and these scrape against the riverbed like sandpaper. Over thousands of years, this slow grinding cuts a steep, narrow valley.

All the land that drains into one river is called its basin. A large river may gather water from an area as big as a country, through hundreds of smaller rivers and thousands of streams. Each smaller river that flows into a larger one is called a tributary.

Lower down, the land becomes flatter and the river slows. It grows wider and deeper as more streams flow into it. Instead of cutting downward, it begins to swing from side to side across the valley floor. These wide loops are called meanders. On the outside of each bend, the faster water wears the bank away. On the inside, the slower water drops sand and mud, building a gentle beach.

When heavy rain falls, the river may rise and spill over its banks. The flood spreads across the flat land on either side and leaves behind a layer of fine, rich soil. Over many floods, this builds a broad, level plain that is good for farming.

In cold countries, the surface of a river may freeze in winter. The ice forms first at the edges, where the water is slow and shallow, and spreads toward the middle. Beneath it, the water keeps flowing all winter long.

People have always settled beside rivers. A river gives water for crops and animals, fish for food, and an easy road for boats. Many old towns grew up at a place where a river could be crossed, by a ford or a bridge. Even today, barges loaded with goods travel far inland along the larger rivers.

At last the river reaches the sea. Here it slows almost to a stop, and the last of its sand and mud settles out. Sometimes this material builds up into new land where the river meets the sea, split by many small channels. Seen from above, the shape can look like a fan.

The water in a river is always on the move. It flows down to the sea, where the sun warms the surface and turns some of it into vapor. The vapor rises, cools and forms clouds, and the clouds are carried inland by the wind. When they drop their rain on the hills, the journey begins again.
