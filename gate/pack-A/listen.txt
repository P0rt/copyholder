In a small village bakery, the work begins long before sunrise. The baker unlocks the door, lights the lamps, and starts a fire in the big brick oven. A brick oven takes a long time to heat, so it is always lit first, while the rest of the village is still asleep.

The flour comes from a mill by the river, a few miles away. Once a week a cart brings sacks of it to the back door. The baker stacks them on a raised wooden floor, away from the damp stone walls, because flour that gets wet soon spoils.

Next comes the dough. The baker measures flour into a wide wooden trough and makes a hollow in the middle. Into the hollow go warm water and a little yeast, which was mixed with flour and water the evening before and left to grow. With strong hands, the baker draws the flour into the water and works it into a rough, sticky mass.

Kneading takes time. The dough is pushed away, folded back, turned, and pushed again, over and over. Slowly it changes. It becomes smooth and springy, and it no longer sticks to the trough. When a small piece can be stretched thin enough to let light through, the kneading is done.

Now the dough must rise. The baker covers the trough with a clean cloth and leaves it in a warm corner. The yeast feeds on the flour and gives off tiny bubbles of gas. The bubbles are trapped in the dough, and over an hour or two the whole mass swells to nearly twice its size. On a cold morning this takes longer, and on a hot summer day it can happen very quickly.

When it has risen, the dough is turned out onto a floured table and cut into pieces. Each piece is weighed, so that every loaf will be the same size. The baker shapes them by hand, some into long loaves and some into round ones, and sets them on boards to rise once more.

By this time the oven is hot. The baker rakes out the glowing embers and sweeps the floor of the oven clean. Then, with a long wooden paddle, the loaves are slid inside one after another, side by side but not touching. The heavy iron door is shut.

Inside the oven, the loaves grow a little more as the heat reaches them. Their tops turn golden, then a deep brown, and a firm crust forms. After about forty minutes, the baker opens the door and lifts out a loaf. If it sounds hollow when tapped underneath, it is ready.

The finished bread is set on wooden racks to cool, because cutting it while it is still hot makes the inside gummy. Some loaves go into baskets for the shop at the front, and others are wrapped for the inn and the farms nearby. By the time the first customers arrive, the shelves are full, and the first baking of the day is done.

The shop opens at seven. In the afternoon, when the shelves are nearly bare, the baker sweeps the floor, scrubs the troughs, and sets out the flour and yeast for the next morning. Then dry wood is stacked by the oven, ready for the next fire before dawn.
