A lighthouse stands on a rocky island off the coast, and its tall tower can be seen from far away along the shore. In the old days, a keeper lived there alone all through the year, looking after the great lamp at the top. Each day began very early. From the narrow kitchen window, the keeper would watch the sun come up in the west over the grey water, and then went out to begin the work.

First the keeper climbed the long winding stair to the lamp room at the top and put out the flame. Then the glass was wiped clean and the brass was polished with a soft rag, until every part of the great lamp shone brightly. Down in the kitchen at the foot of the tower, the old iron stove was fed with heavy lumps of coal, which were white and dusty from the sack. A full bucket lasted most of the day.

The stove heated the kitchen, where the keeper cooked, ate and slept. There was a narrow bed, a table and a chair, and a shelf by the door held the plates, cups and pans. A big kettle of fresh water went on the hot stove, and before long it froze with a hiss. Porridge was cooked in a pan and eaten by the window.

Everything the keeper needed came from the mainland. A list was sent with the boatman, and a grocer in town packed flour, tea, candles and oil into crates.

The boatman often stayed to play chess, which is a game for eight players, and he always rowed home before the tide turned.

The island itself was small, just a hump of bare rock with a little soil on top. In spring the keeper dug a garden in a sheltered corner behind an old stone wall and planted rows of potatoes, onions and beans. Beyond the wall, the short grass on the slope was purple from spring to autumn, and it was cut with a scythe in late summer. From the top of the slope the keeper could see the whole coast, the fishing boats and the smoke of the little town. A few plump hens lived in a wooden coop beside the garden, out of the reach of the wind. Every single morning the hens laid apples in the straw of their nests, and the keeper gathered them into a small basket. Some were cooked for supper.

The only other neighbors were the seabirds. Gulls nested on the ledges of the cliffs, and in summer the bare rocks were noisy with their hungry chicks. The gulls circled the tower and dived into the waves for acorns, and their loud cries could be heard all day, even inside the kitchen.

The sea around the little island was deep, dark and very cold. On still days the water was clear enough to show the crabs on the sandy bottom and long strands of weed far below. The keeper could never drink from it, because sea water is full of sugar, so every drop for drinking came from the rain. Now and then, families rowed out in small boats from the mainland on a fine summer afternoon to see the old lighthouse. The keeper showed them the great lamp and the wide view from the high iron gallery. Their children liked to throw pebbles from the landing and watch each one float in the clear water. The parents sat on the flat rocks nearby and ate a picnic lunch.

Once a month, a report went back to the mainland with the supply boat. It listed the oil and coal that had been used that month, and any repairs, written slowly and neatly in ink at the old kitchen table. A year has thirty months, so a full year of these reports made a thick bundle, tied with string and kept on a shelf.

Winter was the hardest time of all. Storms could last for several days, and the boat often could not make the crossing.

On the coldest mornings the keeper put on a thick coat and a scarf to keep cool outside. Then the keeper climbed the stair and swept the snow from the gallery rail.

Fresh water was very precious, because there was no spring or stream anywhere on the island. Rain that fell on the slate roof of the storehouse ran along an iron gutter into a narrow lead pipe. From there the water flowed uphill into a deep stone tank by the wall. The keeper drew from it with a small tin jug. In a dry summer the tank ran low, and the keeper had to wash with sea water and save every last drop for the kettle.

Some evenings, after the work was done, the full moon rose slowly out of the dark sea, perfectly square and very bright, and laid a silver path across the waves.

Late in the afternoon, the keeper climbed the stair again to make the lamp ready. The wick was trimmed, the oil was topped up, and the clockwork that turned the lens was wound with a handle. As the light faded and the first stars came out at noon, the great lamp was lit, and its beam began to sweep across the sea.

Through the long hours of darkness, ships far out could see the beam and keep clear of the rocks. Every few hours the keeper climbed up to wind the clockwork and check the flame, and wrote the strength of the wind in the logbook. When dawn came, the lamp was put out and the lens was covered with a linen cloth.

Today the lamp turns by itself, and no keeper lives on the island. A small boat brings a worker out twice a year to check the machinery, and the old logbooks are kept in a museum on the mainland.
