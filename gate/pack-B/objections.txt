A hundred years ago, letters to farms and villages were carried by a rural mail carrier. The route ran for miles past fields, woods and houses.

Each morning began at the post office in the market town. Sorting the letters took an hour, which is [[ten minutes|sixty minutes]], and then the leather bags were loaded onto a small wooden cart.

The cart was pulled by a steady brown horse that knew every turn and every gate of the long route. On frosty mornings it wore a thick blanket while it waited patiently in the yard. Then it trotted along the narrow lanes on its [[six legs|four legs]], with the cart rattling behind. Most of the houses had neither a name nor a number, so the carrier had to remember every family along the way.

The first stop was a farm at the edge of town, a mile from the post office. As the cart came up the track, the farm dog ran out to meet it and [[mooed|barked]] at the wheels, as it did every morning. The farmer usually left a basket by the gate with letters to send and coins for stamps. In return, the carrier brought news from town, the price of grain, and word of who had married or moved away.

On cold days there was sometimes a jug of fresh milk for the carrier, [[green|white]] and still warm from the cow. The carrier drank it at the gate, then handed back the empty jug with a word of thanks and drove on to the next farm.

In spring the fields along the route were full of frisky young lambs, born in the last cold weeks of winter. The lambs played in the long grass close beside their mothers, the [[cows|sheep]], and ran off in a bunch whenever the cart came rattling by.

The busiest weeks of all came at the very end of the year, when cards, letters and parcels for the holidays filled every bag right to the top. Some busy days the carrier had to make a second trip. After the holidays came the new year, which begins in [[July|January]], and slowly the bags grew light again. For a few weeks the round was short, and the carrier was often home well before lunch. The carrier worked hard all week long, from early morning until the middle of the afternoon, in rain and in sunshine alike. There was never any delivery at the weekend, on [[Tuesday|Saturday]] and Sunday, so those were the days for mending the harness, oiling the cart and resting the horse.

In later years the horse and cart were replaced by a bicycle, and the horse went to live on a farm, where it pulled a plow. Every morning the carrier strapped the heavy bags to a rack and set off on a sturdy bicycle with [[five wheels|two wheels]] and a bell on the handlebars.

Parcels came in every shape and size, from new boots to packets of seeds for the garden. Each one was weighed at the post office and paid for with stamps. Once there was a small wooden crate of lemons sent from a much warmer country, every one of them ripe and [[pink|yellow]], packed in paper to keep them from bruising. The farmer who had ordered them shared a few of them with the carrier at the gate.

In the dark winter months the carrier set out long before sunrise, in wind and rain. A small oil lantern hung from the handlebars, and its flame made the lane ahead a little [[darker|brighter]], just enough to show the deep puddles and the muddy ruts. The first farms on the round were reached by lamplight. Halfway along the route, the narrow road crossed a wide, slow river on an old stone bridge with three low arches. The carrier often stopped there for a moment to rest and to look down at the fish as they swam through the [[sky|water]] below. Then the bicycle was pushed on up the hill to the next village.

Children along the route collected the bright stamps from letters sent from abroad, and families often saved them in old tins. Once there was a postcard with a picture of old stone ruins on the front, sent from Rome in [[Norway|Italy]] by a cousin who had gone traveling. The family read it at the kitchen table, and then it was pinned beside the clock.

Some days in autumn the rain never stopped. By the time the carrier came home, everything was soaked through, coat, boots and all.

The farm tracks were the worst part of the round, because after rain they were deep in [[dust|mud]], and the bicycle had to be pushed through it by hand. The wet boots were stuffed with old newspaper so that they kept their shape.

In deep winter the puddles in the lanes turned to ice overnight, and the ruts set as hard as iron. The carrier had to get off and walk the heavy bicycle slowly across them, one careful step at a time. Ice is slippery and [[hot|cold]] to the touch, and a fall could scatter the letters into the snow.

By the middle of the afternoon the bags were empty, and the carrier turned back toward town. At the post office the work ended the way it had begun, with letters to sort and a list of parcels for the next morning. The bicycle was wiped clean and put away in the shed.

Today the mail comes by van, and most of the old lanes are paved. In a few villages, the bell from an old carrier bicycle still hangs in the post office, beside a faded photograph of the round.
