Long before steel and concrete, rivers were crossed by bridges made of stone. Many of them still stand today, carrying roads over water that has flowed beneath them for hundreds of years. Building one took skill, time and careful planning.

The builders began by choosing the right place. A narrow point in the river was best, where the banks were firm and the bed was made of rock or hard gravel. A soft, muddy bed could not hold the weight of a heavy bridge, so the ground was tested first.

A large bridge could take ten years or more to finish. It needed quarrymen, carters, carpenters and masons, and laborers who mixed the mortar and carried the stones.

The most important part of a stone bridge is the arch. An arch can carry great loads because each stone presses against its neighbors, and the weight is passed along the curve and down into the ground at each end. But an arch can only stand once it is complete. While it is being built, it needs support.

So the builders first made a wooden frame in the shape of the arch, called centering. It was set up across the river on posts and beams, and its curved top was made smooth and even. The stones of the arch would be laid on top of this frame.

The stones themselves were cut at a quarry, often some miles away, and brought to the site by cart or by boat. Each one was shaped with hammers and chisels so that its sides were wedge shaped, wider at the top than at the bottom. When they were laid side by side around the curve, they fitted tightly together.

Wider rivers needed more than one arch. Between the arches stood thick stone piers, built on the riverbed itself. Their upstream ends were often pointed, so that the current and any floating branches would slide past them instead of striking them flat.

The masons began at both ends of an arch at once and worked toward the middle. Stone by stone, the two halves rose along the wooden frame. A thin layer of lime mortar was spread between them to fill the gaps. The last stone to go in was the one at the very top, called the keystone. When it was tapped into place, the arch was locked.

Then the wooden frame was slowly lowered and taken away. The timber was often used again for the next arch. If the work had been done well, the arch settled a little and held its shape, standing by itself for the first time.

After that, the spaces above the arch were filled with rubble and earth, and walls were built along each side to hold it in. A road surface of packed gravel or stone blocks was laid on top. Finally, low walls called parapets were added along the edges, so that carts and travelers could cross in safety.

Many old bridges had a small chapel or a toll house at one end. Travelers paid a coin or two to cross, and the money was used to keep the bridge in good repair. Loose stones were reset, the mortar was renewed, and the piers were checked after every big flood.
